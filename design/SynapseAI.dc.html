The empirical record is mixed, and the split is mostly about method

Modern studies do not find the large employment losses predicted by a simple competitive labour market model. Card and Krueger's 1994 New Jersey–Pennsylvania comparison found no significant employment decline in fast food after a wage increase, and later replications using administrative payroll data reached similar conclusions at moderate wage floors.

The 2017 Seattle study is often cited as the counterexample; it reported a reduction in hours worked, not in the number of people employed — the draft answer conflated the two, and the verifier corrected it.

Two distinctions decide most disagreements: short-run versus long-run effects, and how far the new floor sits above the local median wage. Commonly quoted elasticities of −0.1 to −0.3 could not be traced to a single authoritative source; published estimates span roughly −0.5 to +0.1.

Employment elasticity, as used above:

ε = (ΔE / E) ÷ (ΔW / W)
bunching-estimator.py
missing = jobs_below_new_floor.sum()
excess  = jobs_at_or_above_floor.sum()
net     = excess - missing   # ≈ 0 in Cengiz et al.
  • Moderate increases: employment effects near zero in most credible designs.
  • Large increases relative to local median: evidence thins and uncertainty widens.
  • Hours, scheduling and turnover move before headcount does.
